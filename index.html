What it does

Every Facility Response Plan carries a list of who to call during a spill: names, titles, phone numbers, organizations. Those lists decay quietly. People change jobs and die, organizations dissolve, numbers get reassigned, and none of it costs anything until the day of a spill or the day of an inspection.

The tool takes a facility's notification list, runs verification checks against it, and produces a short branded report flagging the entries that are stale, unreachable, or unverifiable. It is the entry product: cheap to deliver, it shows a prospect the real state of their plan, and the findings map directly onto the paid work (digitization, notification modernization, tabletop exercises, drills).

Why the pitch holds up

In an EPA field inspection of the Greater Cincinnati Asphalt Terminal (FRP0500295, North Bend OH), the inspector wrote, verbatim: "Some telephone numbers were incorrect. Please update the notification list." The facility was given 60 days to resubmit. That plan carries a 38.8 mile planning distance and a 6,000,000 gallon worst case discharge.

Every one of those figures was checked against the inspection document itself rather than taken secondhand. The same inspection raised two further findings unrelated to the contact list. This is a documented case, not an anecdote.

How an audit runs

  1. Describe the facility. Name, state, FRP identifier, plan revision date. The state drives the registry and area code checks; the revision date drives the staleness check.

  2. Paste in the roles the plan itself requires. Role coverage is an internal consistency check: the plan judged against its own stated requirements, never against our reading of the regulation.

  3. Upload the notification pages. PDF, CSV, or spreadsheet. If someone uploads the EPA inspection report by mistake, the tool notices there is no contact list in it and says so rather than inventing entries.

  4. Confirm every extracted row against the source. Rows pulled from a PDF arrive unconfirmed, and checks will not run until a human has signed off on each. A misread digit produces a false finding, which is worse than no finding.

  5. Run the checks and review every flag. Each entry gets a status, a reason, and a suggested fix, all editable before the customer sees them.

  6. Approve and deliver. Approval locks the audit, signs it with the operator's login, and clears the draft watermark. The report prints to PDF from the browser.

Reading the report

Verified

Every applicable check passed.

Could not verify

Neutral, and not an accusation. Our sources could not confirm the entry. It does not mean the entry is wrong.

Flagged for review

A check returned a specific signal the facility should act on. Every flag carries a reason and a recommended fix.

The rule the whole product rests on. The report flags, it never declares. It never states that a person is deceased, has left a job, or that a number is dead, because we cannot know those things and saying them creates real exposure. This is enforced in the software rather than left to whoever writes the report: every customer-facing sentence is drawn from a fixed library, and if an operator types an assertion into a finding, the tool refuses to save it and asks them to rephrase it as what could or could not be verified.

What you will notice when you try it. Nothing currently reaches "verified" status. The phone, registry, mailbox, and address checks are built and wired but have no vendor API keys behind them yet, so they honestly report that no lookup source is configured. That is the tool refusing to overstate what it knows, and it resolves the moment we buy the data sources.

Decisions already made

  • Anonymized marketing metrics are allowed, but only from audits where the customer gave explicit written consent. Opt-in, recorded per audit, defaults to no, withdrawn by a deletion request, and nothing publishes from a sample under five audits.
  • AI does one job: reading a plan page into structured rows. It never runs verification and never writes a word of the report, because fixed report language is what makes the liability rule enforceable at all. It is off by default and works with any provider.
  • v1 does not do automated outreach, continuous monitoring, a public self-serve product, or any assertion about an individual.

One idea worth ten minutes

EPA Risk Management Plan data is public by statute and carries the same emergency contact fields we audit: name, title, phone, 24-hour phone, email. Facilities are required to correct that contact information within one month of any change. That gives us a public, at-scale way to identify facilities with demonstrably stale emergency contacts without asking anyone for a document. It is a lead list, a marketing statistic, and arguably a second product with a far tighter clock than the FRP five year cycle.
